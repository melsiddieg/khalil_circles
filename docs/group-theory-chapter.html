The Turn That Changes Nothing§1

§1The Move You Cannot See

✦

A ceiling fan has three identical blades. Turn it by exactly one third of a full circle. Each blade moves into its neighbour’s position, and since the blades are identical, the fan looks untouched. A physical act left no visible trace.

The definition:

A symmetry of a thing is a move that leaves it looking exactly as it was.

This defines symmetry as a move, not a property. It doesn’t say the object is balanced or regular — it says: perform an action, and the object can’t tell. A snowflake has sixth-turn symmetry. A square has quarter-turns and flips. A human face has roughly one mirror. To say what symmetry something has is to list the moves it fails to detect.

That shift — from an aesthetic quality to a set of operations — is the starting point of group theory.

The Turn That Changes Nothing§1
§1 · continued

Three properties of these moves follow immediately.

First, doing nothing counts. Leaving the fan alone certainly leaves it unchanged. This is a symmetry, the same way zero is a number.

Second, every move has an inverse. If a third-turn clockwise is a symmetry, so is a third-turn counterclockwise.

Third, moves compose. Turn by a third, then by a third again: the result is a two-thirds turn, and the fan detects neither step. Combining symmetries always produces another symmetry.

These three properties — identity, inverses, closure under composition — define a group. Any set of moves satisfying them is one. The study of such sets is group theory.

The definition is minimal, and its reach is the surprise: it underlies modern physics, chemistry, and cryptography. The examples here are five circles drawn in eighth-century Basra to classify the metres of Arabic poetry.

The Turn That Changes Nothing§2

§2A Group, Properly Met

✦

The fan has exactly three symmetries: stay (do nothing), a ⅓ turn, and a ⅔ turn. A quarter-turn is not one — it leaves the blades visibly askew. A full turn is just stay again.

Since moves compose, the whole system fits in a table: pick a row, follow it by a column, read off the result.

The table certifies all four group axioms. Every entry stays in the set (closure). The stay row copies its header, like adding zero (identity). Each move has a partner that returns to stay: ⅓ and ⅔ undo each other, and stay undoes itself (inverses). And chains can be grouped in any order with the same result (associativity).

This group is the cyclic group of order 3, written C3. Cyclic because one move (the ⅓ turn) generates all the others by repetition. Order 3 because there are three moves.

§2 · continued

Clock arithmetic is a cyclic group. On a twelve-hour face, 9 + 5 = 2: fourteen steps, but the wheel can’t tell from two. That wrapping is C12.

The general construction: place n marks on a wheel; the moves are advance by k for k = 0, 1, …, n−1; composition is addition mod n. This is Cn, the cyclic group of order n — the symmetry group of anything that repeats in a loop.

Cyclic groups are the simplest kind, which is why they work as an introduction. They require nothing beyond a wheel and modular addition. But they already exhibit the phenomena — actions, orbits, stabilizers, conservation laws — that govern much larger groups.

In the eighth century, al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad built five such wheels from the sounds of Arabic poetry and used them to answer a question: how many genuinely different metres does the language permit?

§3The Poet’s Raw Material

✦

Arabic verse is quantitative: metre is a fixed pattern of long and short syllables. The tradition writes it with two marks. A struck note / is a consonant carrying a vowel. A rest 0 is an unvowelled consonant that closes a syllable. The marks read right-to-left, like the script.

These two symbols combine into a small set of units — the pegs and cords that make up every metre:

Al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad al-Farāhīdī (الخليل بن أحمد الفراهيدي)

Born in Oman around 718 CE, working in Basra until his death around 786, al-Khalīl compiled the first Arabic dictionary (Kitāb al-ʿAyn), organised by place of articulation; taught Sībawayh, author of the foundational Arabic grammar; and founded ʿilm al-ʿarūḍ, the science of metre. Tradition says the system came to him from the rhythm of a coppersmith’s hammer in the market.

The most common classical metre is الطويل (aṭ-Ṭawīl, "the long one"). Its half-line is فَعُولُنْ مَفَاعِيلُنْ فَعُولُنْ مَفَاعِيلُنْ — in prosodic notation, the ten-unit sequence 0// 0/ 0// 0/ 0/ repeated twice. The same sequence returns line after line. The pattern repeats.

Al-Khalīl’s move was to close the loop. Join the end of the repeating sequence to its start, and the metre becomes a necklace of ten units.

This is a quotient. Whenever a pattern repeats every n steps, positions that differ by n are equivalent. Identifying them turns an infinite line into a wheel with n marks — the wheel of Fig. 4. The circle is not a diagram of the metre; it is the metre, with repetition factored out.

Five such necklaces cover all of Arabic poetry. The first, دائرة المختلف (the circle of the differing), has ten beads: three pegs and seven cords. A wheel with ten marks carries a group: C10.

The Turn That Changes Nothing§4

§4One Necklace, Many Clasps

✦

A necklace has no beginning, but a metre must start somewhere. Choose a starting point — a clasp — and read around the loop. C10, the ten rotations of a ten-bead wheel, acts on the necklace by moving the clasp from bead to bead.

Move the clasp one bead forward and read again. The same beads, in the same cyclic order, yield a different metre — different feet, different rhythm, a different name in the tradition. Rotation changes what you hear.

When a group’s elements operate on an object this way, the group acts on it. Each element gives a different view of the same object. In modern terms, al-Khalīl’s insight is: the sixteen metres of Arabic poetry are not independent. They are five necklaces, read from different starting points under rotation.

Ten rotations, ten possible clasps. The question is whether all ten yield distinct metres.

Two features of this list need explaining.

The list is short. Ten rotations produced five distinct readings. Position 5 sounds exactly like position 0, position 6 like position 1. Some rotations produce no new reading. Why five? That is the subject of §5 and §6.

The list has gaps. Poets use three of the five: الطويل، المديد، البسيط. The other two are well-formed rhythms nobody adopted. Al-Khalīl named them rather than discarding them. §9 returns to these empty cells.

§6The Stabilizer: The Necklace’s Own Symmetry

✦

Why does position 5 sound the same as position 0? The first circle’s sequence is 0// 0/ 0// 0/ 0/ — repeated twice. Two identical halves. Rotating by five beads sends every peg to a peg and every cord to a cord. The necklace can’t tell the difference. This is the fan’s symmetry, applied to beads.

The moves that leave a necklace unchanged form a subgroup — they compose and invert within themselves. This subgroup is the stabilizer. For circle 1 the stabilizer is { stay, turn-by-5 }: C2.

This explains why the list is short. Applying a stabilizer move to any clasp position leaves the reading unchanged. Ten positions, paired by the stabilizer, give five distinct readings. More symmetry means fewer distinct readings.

The stabilizer can be drawn. Connect each bead to the bead the stabilizer sends it to. The chords form a star polygon, and because the move preserves bead types, every chord joins two beads of the same colour.

The shepherd’s theorem

Forty legs, four per sheep, ten sheep. Divide a total by a uniform redundancy to get a count. This is, almost literally, the theorem on the next page.

One distinction worth making: the group C10 belongs to the wheel — the ten positions. The stabilizer belongs to the beading: thread the same wheel with different beads and the stabilizer changes. Symmetry is a property of the pattern, not the positions.

§7A Conservation Law

✦

Three counts on the first circle: the group has 10 moves, the orbit has 5 readings, the stabilizer has 2 elements.

10  =  5  ×  2 moves  =  distinct readings  ×  symmetries

This is not a coincidence. Sort the ten rotations by which reading they produce. Each pile has exactly the stabilizer’s size: two rotations that give the same reading differ by a stabilizer element. Every pile is a copy of the stabilizer, shifted to a different starting point.

In full generality, this is the orbit–stabilizer theorem: for a finite group acting on an object,

size of group  =  size of orbit  ×  size of stabilizer.

The product is fixed, so symmetry and variety trade off. More symmetry means fewer distinct readings, and vice versa. The group size is the budget; orbit and stabilizer split it.

Read downward, this is five instances of one multiplication. Read across, it is a trade-off. The two middle rows are the interesting case. Both necklaces have nine beads and the same group C9. One yields three readings, the other nine. Same count, different structure. §8 explains the difference.

The Turn That Changes Nothing§8

§8Symmetry Comes in Steps, Not Slopes

✦

Before comparing the two nine-bead circles: the stabilizer’s size must divide the group’s size exactly. Equal piles divide the total with no remainder. (This is Lagrange’s theorem, which holds for every subgroup of every finite group.) The consequence: possible symmetry sizes are not continuous. They are a short list of divisors.

On nine beads, the options are C9 (only if all beads are identical), C3, or C1. Nothing in between. A nine-bead necklace that lacks C3 symmetry doesn’t degrade gracefully — it drops directly to C1, the trivial group. There is no intermediate step.

The two nine-bead circles sit on opposite sides of this gap.

Compare the two nine-bead circles bead by bead:

One bead changed out of nine — the smallest possible alteration. By the divisor ladder, the result is total: C3 drops to C1, orbit 3 becomes orbit 9. The two most similar circles in the system have the greatest difference in symmetry.

Al-Khalīl’s tradition named this circle المشتبه — the resembling, and simultaneously the confusable: in Arabic the word carries both senses. Its nine rotations are near-misses of one another, maximally similar while remaining distinct. (The best-known confusions in practice come from a different source — lossy metrical variations that let one circle’s metre sound like another’s. That is a separate mechanism, covered in the companion chapter on تداخل.)

A note on invariants: the stabilizer records that symmetry is absent — C1, the same as random noise. It doesn’t record how close the necklace came to being symmetric. The group-theoretic invariant loses that information. The traditional name المشتبه preserves it.

The Turn That Changes Nothing§9

§9The Catalogue and Its Gaps

✦

The orbits sum to 5 + 2 + 3 + 9 + 2 = 21 distinct readings across all five circles. Classical poetry uses sixteen. Al-Khalīl kept all twenty-one.

A catalogue built from evidence lists what occurs. A catalogue built from a group action lists what is possible. The orbit doesn’t depend on attestation. The five vacancies are structural predictions: rhythms the system permits, independent of what poets wrote. Absence becomes a fact requiring explanation.

Mendeleev did the same with elements: trust the structure, leave the cells empty, let experiment fill them. Al-Khalīl’s empty cells remain empty — poets are not obliged — but the vacant metres were tried, and المستطيل and الممتد survive in the manuals as named but unused readings. All five gaps fall in the two least symmetric circles, the only ones whose orbits exceeded what the tradition used.

§10The Price of Symmetry

✦

The orbit–stabilizer theorem fixes the product orbit × stabilizer. Across the five circles, the trade-off is visible:

In ordinary language, symmetry is a good thing. In group theory, symmetry is sameness, and sameness reduces variety. Every undetectable turn is a clasp position that yields no new reading. Perfect symmetry would mean one reading — monotony. Distinct things come from broken symmetry.

The same trade-off appears at other scales. The early universe cooled through broken symmetries into the particle zoo. A crystal’s lattice hosts one pattern; a disordered glass hosts many. Al-Khalīl’s circles show it in nine beads: the concordant circle (C3) produces two metres; the resembling circle (C1) produces nine.

The Turn That Changes Nothing§10
§10 · closing

The components assembled here: a set of objects (rhythms); a group acting on them (rotations); an equivalence relation (same reading); the equivalence classes counted (orbits = metres); each object’s symmetry measured (stabilizer); a conservation law (orbit–stabilizer); a complete catalogue derived from structure, not evidence; and predicted absences. This is the classification method of modern mathematics. Al-Khalīl executed every step, by ear, eleven centuries before Galois named the subject.

What the circles show about group theory is that it is not a notation. It is a framework for answering a question: when are two things genuinely the same, and how many genuinely different things exist? Ask that question carefully enough — of molecules, ciphers, or verse — and you will find orbits and stabilizers, whether or not you have names for them. The formalism didn’t create the symmetry of poetry. It gave a name to what a lexicographer had already found.
