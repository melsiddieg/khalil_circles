What Islamic geometric ornament and the metre circles of al-Khalīl have in common — and where they differ
“A mathematician, like a painter or a poet, is a maker of patterns. If his patterns are more permanent than theirs, it is because they are made with ideas.” — G. H. HARDY, A Mathematician’s Apology (1940)
· 1 ·
The Compass and the Ear§1

§1Two Workshops, One Idea

✦

Two crafts, centuries apart. In eighth-century Basra, al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad works out that every classical Arabic metre comes from five circles of repeating sound-units — the subject of chapter I. In a workshop in Isfahan or Fez or Granada, a craftsman sets a compass on a blank panel, divides a circle into equal parts, and lays out the strapwork that will cover a wall with stars.

The two crafts never spoke. One works in sound, the other in tile and plaster. Both rest on the same premise: a small kit of parts, repeated under strict rules, produces unlimited variety. Analysed with modern mathematics, the same structures appear in each — the groups, orbits and stabilizers of chapter I, plus several that exist only in two dimensions.

This chapter is a guided comparison. The route: the shared construction method — a circle that gets erased (§2); a shared vocabulary of stars, including one figure that appears identically in Qurʼan manuscripts and in the metre circles (§3); the big difference — mirrors work in space but not in time, with one computed surprise (§4); strips and grids, where the eye out-scores the ear seven to one and then seventeen to one (§5); the rules each medium imposes, and the famous workaround (§6); why both arts break their own symmetry on purpose (§7); and finally a side-by-side dictionary of the whole correspondence (§8). The claim is structural, not mystical, and every number in it can be checked.

Start with the parts lists. Both are short. Chapter I built every classical metre from four units: the light cord 0/, the joined peg 0//, the split peg /0/, and the cluster 0///. Four bricks, sixteen metres. On the ornament side, when researchers reconstructed how the most intricate five-fold patterns could have been laid out, they arrived at a kit of just five tiles — the girih tiles — all with the same edge length, each carrying the same angled guide-lines. Five shapes, and enough patterns for walls from Baghdad to Bukhara.

A fair objection: every craft has parts, so a short parts list proves nothing by itself. The real link is what both traditions do with their kits. Neither arranges parts freely. Both generate their designs from a circle plus a counting rule — which means the space of possible designs can be listed completely. Every possible metre; every possible star from a given division. This turns a repertoire into a catalogue, and catalogues are what mathematics can analyse. That is the link the rest of the chapter follows.

The Compass and the Ear§2

§2Start with a Circle — Then Hide It

✦

Here is how a geometric panel is actually made. Step one: draw a circle. Step two: divide it into n equal parts. Step three: connect the division points by a counting rule — every second point, every third. Step four: erase the construction. The finished wall shows the star; the circle that produced it is gone. Nearly every Islamic geometric design, however complex it looks, starts exactly this way.

Now compare the prosodist. Chapter I showed that a metre is built the same way: a circle of units, a chosen starting point, the units read off around the loop. And then the circle disappears — no poem ever shows its dāʼira. Listeners get a line of verse; the wheel that generated it appears only in the technical manuals.

So both arts have a two-layer structure. The public layer is the artifact: the wall, the poem. The private layer is the generating circle. The detail that matters: neither tradition discarded its private layer. Both wrote it down, in a specialist literature of diagrams — which means both understood that the circle, not the surface, is where the design really lives. The next page shows the paper trail.

The paper trail

The link between geometers and craftsmen is documented, not guessed. In tenth-century Baghdad, the mathematician Abū al-Wafāʼ al-Būzjānī wrote a practical manual — On the Geometric Constructions Necessary for the Artisan — and mentions working sessions where geometers and craftsmen solved problems together. The Topkapı scroll (studied by Gülru Necipoğlu, 1995) is about thirty metres of construction drawings: a working pattern book that preserves exactly the scaffolding the finished walls hide. The prosodists’ manuals, with their circle diagrams of the sixteen metres, are the same kind of document for poetry.

This two-layer picture also clarifies what “abstract art” means here. The structure comes first; any particular wall or poem is one rendering of it. Change the tiles’ colours and it is still the same pattern; change the poem’s words and it is still الطويل. The circle is the master copy, and both traditions gave the master copy its own literature.

§3A Shared Alphabet of Stars

✦

If the two systems share one visual, it is the star polygon. The recipe: put n points evenly on a circle, then connect every k-th point. The result is written {n/k}. Chapter I met these stars as the drawn form of a symmetry — connect each unit of a metre circle to the unit its rotation-symmetry carries it to, and you get the star {n/p}. Islamic ornament, free to choose any n and k, made the same figures its favourite vocabulary:

One arithmetic fact controls how these stars look: the greatest common divisor gcd(n, k). If gcd(n, k) = 1, the star is a single unbroken path — you can draw {8/3} without lifting the pen. If gcd(n, k) = d > 1, the star splits into d separate interlocking polygons: {6/2} is two triangles, {8/2} is two squares, {9/3} is three triangles. The interlace so typical of Islamic design — separate closed bands woven together — is this divisor arithmetic made visible. (Group theory calls the d bands the cosets of a subgroup; the picture is all you need to keep.)

Now the best single example in the chapter.

These are the same figure. The mark that has divided the text of the Qurʼan for a thousand years is exactly the star that circle 5’s symmetry draws on its eight units. And nobody copied anyone. One practice divides a sacred text into quarters and eighths for recitation; the other divides a circle of sounds into readings. Both perform the same underlying operation — a cycle of eight, stepped through two at a time — so both produce the same shape. That is how a shared mathematics shows itself: not as resemblance, but as identical objects reached from different directions.

The hexagram gives a second example. As ornament it is the khātam — the Seal — one of the oldest devices in the region’s art. As mathematics it is {6/2}. And in chapter I it appeared as the symmetry star of circle 2, where its two triangles even carry meaning: one triangle connects the three pegs, the other the three clusters. Same figure, three jobs.

§4Mirrors: Where the Two Arts Part Ways

✦

Here the two arts diverge, and the difference is the most informative thing in the chapter. Take an eight-fold rosette. You can rotate it an eighth of a turn and it looks unchanged — eight rotations in all. But you can also flip it, across eight mirror lines. Rotations plus reflections make sixteen symmetries: the dihedral group D8. Ornament gets all sixteen, because ornament lives in space, and space has no preferred direction.

Now try to flip a metre. A reflected rhythm is the rhythm played backwards — and you cannot listen backwards. Sound arrives in time order, and time runs one way. So of the sixteen symmetries the eye enjoys on a rosette, the ear can use at most the eight rotations. This is why every group in chapter I was cyclic (Cn) and never dihedral (Dn). It is not a modelling choice. It is a fact about the medium.

Islamic art uses both options. Most patterns have full mirror symmetry; some deliberately do not: pinwheel motifs in brickwork rotate without reflecting — their group is a bare Cn. And when calligraphers do mirror text, in the style called muthannā (a phrase facing its own reflection), they mirror the written image of the words, never the sound. Writing lives in space, so it can be flipped. Speech lives in time, so it cannot.

The metres carry mirror symmetries that can only be seen, never heard. It took ornament’s point of view to find them.

And the one exception is circle 4, المشتبه — the same circle chapter I found had no rotational symmetry at all (the one-peg story). It turns out to have no mirror either. It is the only chiral necklace in the system: asymmetric under every test, in every geometry. The tradition called it “the confusable.” Every measurement we have applied keeps agreeing.

§5Patterns on a Strip, Patterns on a Plane

✦

Between a single rosette (pattern around a point) and a full wall (pattern across a plane) sits the border: pattern along a strip. Strip patterns have been classified completely — there are exactly seven possible symmetry types, the frieze groups. Islamic ornament uses all seven, in manuscript frames, cornice bands, and woven trims.

A line of metred verse is a strip pattern in sound. The foot repeats — فعولن مفاعيلن فعولن مفاعيلن — and repetition is translation: row 1. The other six rows all involve a reflection or a half-turn: playing the strip backwards, or upside down. For rhythm, backwards is unperformable and upside down is not even defined. So of the seven-row catalogue, the ear’s border art occupies exactly one row.

Music does use the forbidden rows — a crab canon plays a melody against its own reversal. But note where that happens: on the page. Notation lays time out as space, and space can be read both ways. It is the musician’s version of muthannā — a mirror applied to the picture of time, since it cannot be applied to time itself.

Now go up one more dimension. A pattern that repeats in two directions — a wallpaper, a pavement, the tiled lower walls of a madrasa — can have exactly seventeen symmetry types: the wallpaper groups, proved complete by Fedorov in 1891 and independently by Pólya in 1924. The famous claim that the Alhambra contains all seventeen is contested: careful counts range from eleven to seventeen, depending on how colour and restoration are treated. But the exact number matters less than the fact behind it. Craftsmen explored a large share of a seventeen-entry catalogue, centuries before anyone knew the catalogue existed — or that it was finite.

Does poetry have anything two-dimensional? Almost. A classical qaṣīda is a stack of lines in one metre, and every line ends on the same rhyme, sometimes for a hundred lines. As pattern, that is a genuine lattice: repeat across (the foot), repeat down (the verse), with one marked column. But it is a lattice with almost no symmetry beyond the two translations — mirrors and turns are still ruled out, for the reasons of §4. Keep the scoreboard in mind: on a point, the eye gets Dn and the ear Cn; on a strip, seven types against one; on the plane, seventeen against a bare grid. Every added dimension widens the eye’s lead, because every added dimension brings more ways to flip — and flipping is the one move sound can never make.

§6Every Medium Has Rules

✦

Chapter I found a law limiting a metre circle’s symmetry: the symmetry’s size must divide the number of beads (Lagrange’s theorem — divisors only, nothing in between). The plane has its own law, and it is more surprising. A pattern that repeats in two directions can contain rotation centres of order 1, 2, 3, 4, and 6 — and nothing else. No five-fold centre. No seven-fold. Ever, in any periodic pattern. This is called the crystallographic restriction.

Notice the trade-off between the two rulebooks. The circle of beads is generous about size — al-Khalīl’s circles use 6, 8, 9, and 10 beads without trouble — but strict about which symmetries fit a given size (divisors only). The plane is generous about size and strict about kind: whatever the pattern, five-fold symmetry is banned outright. That ban mattered, because Islamic artists loved five- and ten-pointed stars. The next page shows the two ways they got them anyway.

The first workaround is straightforward: put the ten-pointed star inside a local medallion and let the wall’s actual repetition obey the rules. The star is local; the lattice stays legal. Hundreds of buildings use this compromise.

The second workaround is the well-known one. In the girih tradition of Iran, large patterns were built by subdividing big tiles into smaller copies of the same shapes. Subdivision does not need a repeating cell, so it quietly steps around the restriction. At the Darb-i Imam shrine in Isfahan (1453), the result comes remarkably close to a quasicrystalline pattern: five-fold order that never repeats — the structure Roger Penrose discovered mathematically in the 1970s.

The claims and the caveats, in order. Peter Lu and Paul Steinhardt published the quasicrystal reading of Darb-i Imam in Science in 2007. Emil Makovicky had argued a related case for the 1197 Maragha tomb tower years earlier. Peter Cromwell and others have pushed back, favouring traditional construction methods with no quasi-periodic intent. And the standard qualifier is “nearly perfect” — the physical pattern has defects. What nobody disputes is the mathematics: patterns of this family are real, they achieve five-fold order by giving up periodicity rather than law, and girih craftsmen were building convincing approximations of them five centuries before Penrose. The restriction said five-fold order cannot repeat. The craftsmen found the sense in which order does not need to.

§7Why Perfect Symmetry Is Boring

✦

In a tiled interior the eye does not settle on the perfectly repeating field; that reads as background. It goes to where the repetition breaks — the miḥrāb, the central medallion, the calligraphy band. Designers use symmetry as ground and controlled symmetry-breaking as figure. The repetition sets an expectation and the break spends it.

Arabic prosody works exactly the same way. The metre is the ground: fixed, predictable, the listener’s baseline. The ziḥāf system — the licensed foot-variations mapped in this project’s confusion-map view — is the figure: a syllable quieted here, a foot lightened there, always inside strict limits. A poem with zero variation sounds mechanical; one with too much loses the metre. Good poets, like good tilers, worked the boundary between the two.

Chapter I said the same thing with numbers. Its “inverse law” showed that the most symmetric circle produces the fewest metres: symmetry is sameness, and sameness collapses options. Anything you actually notice — a metre, a medallion, a memorable line — exists because some symmetry was deliberately given up. Both arts learned this in practice, long before there was a theory to say it in.

Two mismatches are worth naming, because the table is honest about them. Ornament has nothing like the muhmal metres — patterns that are lawful but that a canon declined to use — because walls have no canon of sixteen. And metre has nothing like the quasicrystal: a one-dimensional loop simply has no room for that kind of order. So the picture is not “two arts, secretly identical.” It is: a large shared core, plus one private wonder on each side. That is what “one mathematics, two instruments” actually means.

§9Two Instruments, One Method

✦

A compass makes equal things: every point it draws sits at the same distance from the centre. An ear detects them: two durations, two stresses, alike or not. One instrument constructs symmetry, the other verifies it. Give both the same task — repeatable pattern under strict rules — and each maps out whatever mathematics its medium allows.

The compass had the larger territory: seventeen wallpaper groups, free mirrors, and the quasi-periodic loophole at the edge. The ear had less — cyclic groups only, one frieze row, a thin lattice. But al-Khalīl did something the artisans never needed to: he worked his catalogue out completely and showed it was closed. Twenty-one rotations, sixteen in use, five named or noted. The artisans explored their catalogue. Al-Khalīl mapped his.

Both fit their own culture’s theory of beauty, too. Ibn al-Haytham’s Book of Optics locates beauty not in objects but in relations between parts — proportion, order, composition. A tiling and a metre are beautiful in precisely that register. The civilization had a theory of why lawful pattern pleases, and built two major arts that run on it.

What this chapter does not claim

No secret geometry of the poets, no numerology of the tilers, no hidden transmission. Craftsmen thought in workshop recipes; prosodists thought in sounds and licenses. The claim is structural, which makes it checkable: examined with modern tools, both repertoires turn out to be organized by the same mathematical objects — group actions, orbits, stabilizers, symmetry laws. Each craft obeyed its medium, and the media obey mathematics. The convergence is the finding.

§10The Names Came Later

✦

Chapter I ended with a date: the 1830s, when Galois named the group — a thousand years after al-Khalīl used one. This chapter adds two more dates. Fedorov proved the seventeen wallpaper groups in 1891; the Alhambra’s builders had explored much of that catalogue by the 1300s. Penrose found aperiodic five-fold order in the 1970s; the Darb-i Imam girih approximated it in 1453.

Three times, the same sequence: practice first, mathematics later. When a craft commits over generations to one medium and strict rules, the structure of that medium shows up in the work, whether or not anyone has words for it. The mathematics in this chapter was not applied to these two arts. It was recovered from them.

Chapter takeaways

• Both arts generate their works from a divided circle that the finished piece hides (§2).
• Star polygons {n/k} are shared vocabulary — and ۞ is literally circle 5’s symmetry star (§3).
• Space allows mirrors; time does not. Ornament gets Dn, metre gets Cn (§4).
• Computed here: four of the five metre circles are secretly mirror-symmetric; المشتبه alone is chiral (§4).
• Strips: 7 symmetry types for the eye, 1 for the ear. Planes: 17 against a bare lattice (§5).
• Each medium has its own law — divisors for bead-circles, {1,2,3,4,6} for the plane — and girih artists bypassed the plane’s law by dropping periodicity, not order (§6).
• In both arts, what you notice is where the symmetry breaks (§7).

The prosodist turning a circle of sounds and the craftsman turning a compass were working on the same object — the group — from opposite sides of the divide between time and space. Neither could see the other’s side. Twelve centuries later the two halves fit.
